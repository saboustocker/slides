Origin Ambiguity

Working Definition:

Origin Ambiguity: The blurring of provenance that occurs when AI-generated content resemble human-created content so closely that people struggle to tell who (or what) made them.

  • of note: ambiguity can also happen elsewhere, for example if you wonder if the content is fake

Origin Inference: A cognitive shift in which people prioritize identifying the source of a work (human or AI) over engaging with its meaning, quality, or message.

  • so what are we talking about?
  • people perceive some sort of origin ambiguity, which leads to engaging with the question who did this more than engaging with the question what was done

Origin Ambiguity

People want to know the origin, but few are able to identify it

  • participants are about as good as chance (Vukojičić, Krstić, and Veinović 2023) identifying AI-generated images
  • and slightly worse than chance (Porter and Machery 2024) to slightly above chance with training at identifying text (Milička et al. 2025)
  • mention quote explicitly: *almost 50% of respondents can distinguish between AI and human** generated artwork.
  • “almost 50% of respondents can distinguish between AI and human generated artwork.” (Vukojičić, Krstić, and Veinović 2023)
  • “participants performed below chance levels in identifying AI-generated poems (46.6% accuracy)” (porterAIgeneratedPoetryIndistinguishable2024a?)
  • “participants were more likely to judge AI-generated poems as human-authored than actual human-authored poems” (porterAIgeneratedPoetryIndistinguishable2024a?)
  • With training, participants were able to correctly identify AI generated text correctly 65% of the time; without training 55% (Milička et al. 2025)

Theoretical Background

Preferences

  • humans \(>\) algorithms / “AI” (Bigman 2022; Kirk and Givi 2025; Lefkeli, Karataş, and Gürhan-Canli 2024, and others)
  • AI-generated content lowers purchase intentions and willingness to recommend a product (Kirk and Givi 2025; Belanche et al. 2025, and others)
  • AI-generated content decreases trust (Kirk and Givi 2025; Pfeuffer et al. 2025; Belanche et al. 2025)

But

  • negative effects are reduced if content is perceived as authentic regardless of origin (Silver, Newman, and Small 2021; Whittaker et al. 2025, and others)

  • It should also be acknowledged that Hartmann, Exner, and Domdey (2025) find that AI images are as good or better in terms of aestethics, realism, and quality, but this does not imply people LIKE them better

  • Humans generally prefer other humans over algorithms & AI (Bigman 2022; Kirk and Givi 2025; Lefkeli, Karataş, and Gürhan-Canli 2024, and others)

  • People are less willing to purchase products when communication is perceived to be written by AI (e.g., Kirk and Givi 2025), imagery is created by AI (e.g., Belanche et al. 2025)

  • Disclosure of use of AI decreases trust (Pfeuffer et al. 2025; Kirk and Givi 2025; Schilke and Reimann 2025)

  • humans alter their behavior in interaction with AI compared to other humans (Goergen, De Bellis, and Klesse 2025; Lefkeli, Karataş, and Gürhan-Canli 2024, and others)

  • preference for humans over AI is mitigated when AI-content is perceived as authentic (Silver, Newman, and Small 2021; Whittaker et al. 2025, and others)

Theoretical Background

Ambiguity Resolution in Marketing

  • ambiguity aversion \(\rightarrow\) preference for established products (Muthukrishnan, Wathieu, and Xu 2009)

  • ambiguous product quality

    \(\rightarrow\) reliance on expectations instead of experience (Yi 1993)

    \(\rightarrow\) more reliance on information from ads (Hoch and Ha 1986)

  • ambiguity in ads grabs attention (Kovacheva and Nikolova 2024; Peracchio and Meyers-Levy 1994; Johar 1995; Hagtvedt 2011; Sevilla and Meyer 2020) …

  • … especially when people are motivated to resolve this ambiguity for one reason or another (Johar 1995; Peracchio and Meyers-Levy 1994; Hagtvedt 2011)

  • ambiguity aversion also occurs in marketing: preference for established products under ambiguous conditions (Muthukrishnan, Wathieu, and Xu 2009)
  • ambiguous product quality leads to evaluation based on top-down expectations instead of bottom-up data-driven approach (Yi 1993)
  • ambiguous product quality leads to higher reliance on ads (Hoch and Ha 1986)
  • ambiguity as an attention grab works (Kovacheva and Nikolova 2024; Peracchio and Meyers-Levy 1994; Johar 1995; Hagtvedt 2011; Sevilla and Meyer 2020), but only when people are motivated to resolve this ambiguity for one reason or another (Johar 1995; Peracchio and Meyers-Levy 1994; Hagtvedt 2011)

Theoretical Background

Cognitive Tax of Ambiguity

  • ambiguity introduces cognitive taxes (Franceschetti et al. 2024; Shin, Kim, and Biocca 2019; Tao et al. 2022; Howard 2017)

    \(\rightarrow\) cognitive taxes have a negative effect on evidence encoding and ability to retain information (e.g., Shou and Smithson 2015)

  • ambiguous stimuli (uncanny valley) introduce negative affect (Mori, MacDorman, and Kageki 2012; Seyama and Nagayama 2007, and others) and tax cognitive resources (Shin, Kim, and Biocca 2019; Tao et al. 2022; Howard 2017)

  • furthermore, there is evidence that different personality types have different needs to resolve ambiguous and obfuscated materials (Guigon, Villeval, and Dreher 2024; Kristofferson and Dunn 2023; Iannello et al. 2017)

  • we do know that it is not human

    • The uncanny valley hypothesis predicts a negative emotional appraisal of human replicas that appear or behave not quite human (Mori, MacDorman, and Kageki 2012)

  • ambiguous stimuli introduce negative affect (Mori, MacDorman, and Kageki 2012; Seyama and Nagayama 2007, and others) …

  • … and tax cognitive resources (Shin, Kim, and Biocca 2019; Tao et al. 2022; Howard 2017)

Intended Contribution

  • combination of streams of research: ambiguity in marketing (materials), cognitive load theory, and consumer-reaction to AI-generated content
  • relevance for both consumer-perspective and marketer-perspective:
    • does AI-generated content harm informed decision-making?
    • is AI-generated content effective?
  • combination of previously disjointed streams of research on ambiguity in marketing, on cognitive load theory, and on consumer exposure and reactions to AI-generated content.
  • Extending prior work on AI where origin is known or the variable in question, we study cases where humans face content that is neither declared AI or human or easily determined as one or the other
  • implications for both marketers and consumers: For marketers, potential backfiring of AI generated ads as well as the resulting trade-offs with efficiency and effectiveness are relevant. For consumers, cognitive resource reallocation might come at the cost of informed decision-making.

\(\rightarrow\) Students in the ESF course are asked to scroll on TikTok and are asked how they perceive their media feed after.

Dimensions:

  1. Origin ambiguity: To what extent do students question the origin of content?
  2. Ambiguity Outcomes: To what extent does ambiguity influence enjoyment, trust, attention focus?
  3. Differentiation: To what extent is “realness vs. fakeness” different from “human-made vs. AI generated”?

\(\rightarrow\) Demonstrate the occurrence and relevance of origin ambiguity in real life through existing correlational data

Possible Avenues:

  1. What type of content is most susceptible to being questioned? Systematic searches on TikTok, Instagram (and co.) and subsequent classification
  2. What type of user is most likely to question the origin?
  3. Are these effect content- or platform-specific?

\(\rightarrow\) Demonstrate downstream effects of origin ambiguity through an experimental setup

Possible Downstream Effects:

  1. Demonstrate reduced recall of content when participants are shown ambiguous marketing material
  2. Demonstrate heightened attention (through eye-tracking, behavior tracking, …) when participants are exposed to ambiguous marketing material
  3. …?

Possible Setups:

  1. One-by-Two Designs: low vs. high origin
  2. Naturalistic designs: DICE (Roggenkamp, Boegershausen, and Hildebrand 2025) to simulate more naturalistic environment
  3. …?

\(\rightarrow\) Harness collaboration with real-life institution or industry partner to assess effects of origin ambiguity in real life

Possible Avenues:

  1. Impressions, Clickthrough rates etc. of real-ads (A/B testing)
  2. Recall of information for low vs. high origin ambiguity in target groups (e.g. through subscriptions, mailinglist, …)
