Shaping my Algorithmic Consumer

Consequences of perceived monitoring and updating of algorithmic identity on consumer behavior and cognition

Author

Sabou Rani Stocker

Published

March 3, 2026

Summary

Living in a digital world, we interact with vast amounts of data every day. By the end of 2025, Instagram was estimated to have had 34 billion posts uploaded in that year alone (Go Beyond 2024). Amazon lists approximately 350 million products (Capital One Shopping 2025), and about 360 hours of content are uploaded to YouTube every minute (moodive 2025). Given this scale, consumers cannot realistically navigate these systems without algorithmic recommendations.

These recommendations are formed through continuous observation and the updating of models of user behavior. As a result, consumers’ cognition and behavior are shaped by perceived monitoring and by the resulting algorithmic representations of the self. Interaction with recommender systems embeds individuals in ongoing processes of data collection and inference, often conceptualized as dataveillance. Awareness of such observation can affect perceived autonomy and induce behavioral adjustment.

Consumers rely on folk theories about how algorithms work, using them to interpret personalized outputs and guide their interactions. These interpretations shape how recommendations are evaluated and whether users align with or resist system inferences.

The dynamic interplay between algorithmic representations and self-concept creates a feedback loop: behavior informs algorithmic models, which in turn shape subsequent preferences and actions. This reciprocal process underlies the central research question of how constant algorithmic observation and model updating affect consumers’ cognitive and behavioral reactions.

As digital systems evolve toward more conversational and adaptive interfaces, AI-mediated recommendations provide a valuable framing device for exploring these dynamics. In AI-mediated environments, where large language models and semi-autonomous agents interpret and act on user information, the salience of observation and the construction of algorithmic selves become central to understanding how constant monitoring and model updating impact consumer cognition and behavior, in particular in the formation of “their algorithmic consumer”. This possible research angle based on general recommender systems literature is referenced under the callouts “AI mediated environments”.

The goal is to assess in our discussion where and how AI mediated consumption as a framing angle is feasible. The AI mediated environments angle is different from other literature, as it focuses on consumer self-perception and curation of an algorithmic self from the consumer perspective, whereas Hermann, Puntoni, and Schweidel (2026) and others focused on the implications for marketers. The idea aims to be the “consumer research” side to the “marketing side” of the discussed coin in the conceptual paper by Hermann, Puntoni, and Schweidel (2026).

In light of recent developments in marketing and consumer research (Hermann, Puntoni, and Schweidel 2026), these consequences become increasingly pertinent to consumers’ everyday lives. With the introduction and rapid diffusion of large language models (LLMs) as a novel interface for accessing information online—and, in a more advanced form, (partially) autonomous digital assistants—consumers are beginning to assume a more secondary role in consumption processes. Instead of directly interacting with providers of goods and services, an “algorithmic consumer” may mediate search, evaluation, and, in some cases, interaction with other agents on their behalf.

This secondary role differs fundamentally from traditional interactions with recommender systems, such as scrolling through Amazon or browsing a personalized feed. In conventional settings, personalization is largely inferred from behavioral traces (e.g., clicks, purchases, browsing history), and consumers engage with the platform without explicitly articulating who they are or what they value. In contrast, LLM-mediated interactions frequently require consumers to provide self-descriptive information, most often in natural language. When asking for advice or recommendations, users must disclose preferences, constraints, goals, or even aspects of their personality. Thus, the consumer is not only selecting among options but actively constructing a representation of themselves that the system uses to generate outputs.

This representational dynamic intensifies as system autonomy increases. In conversational LLM contexts, consumers remain the primary decision-makers, using the model as an advisory interface. However, in the case of more autonomous agents that can evaluate alternatives and interact with providers on the consumer’s behalf, the stakes of self-representation become higher. As delegation increases, so does the importance of how the consumer is modeled within the system, since this representation may directly guide search, evaluation, and potentially transactional behavior. Consequently, the need to consider how one is “represented by the algorithm” becomes more salient as systems move from advisory tools to semi-autonomous actors.

Understanding these dynamics has important implications for both consumer experience and marketing strategy.

Living under Constant Observation

  • Summary

  • AI mediated Environments

Previous research demonstrates that consumers are well aware of algorithmic recommender systems and recognize that their digital experiences are substantially shaped by them (source, source, source). However, the perception of being continuously observed and catered to is not consistently salient in everyday interactions (e.g., Ham 2017). The broader perception of being persistently monitored and leaving data traces in digital environments is commonly referred to as dataveillance (e.g., Ruckenstein and Granroth 2020).

Although dataveillance is frequently portrayed as beneficial by promising more personalized, tailored, and relevant consumer experiences, consumers tend to exhibit aversion to the feeling of being surveilled (Ruckenstein and Granroth 2020; Zhang et al. 2025, among others).

A specific form of dataveillance, namely observation by external actors (typically state authorities), has been shown to induce self-censorship and chilling effects (Büchi, Festic, and Latzer 2022). While chilling effects are most commonly discussed in the context of state surveillance, the underlying mechanism rests on the perception that seemingly minor or inconsequential actions (e.g., publicly criticizing healthcare systems) may lead to disproportionate downstream consequences, such as jeopardizing a student visa (Büchi, Festic, and Latzer 2022). Self-censoring responses to perceived observation are not limited to state surveillance contexts. For instance, similar effects have been documented in domains such as health monitoring devices, where individuals adjust their behavior in light of being tracked (Demetrovics, Ballester-Ripoll, and Billieux 2025). More generally, research shows that the mere presence of observers can reduce perceived decision-making autonomy (Zwebner and Schrift 2020), even in the absence of clearly articulated negative consequences.

In AI-mediated environments, the perception of observation assumes a novel and qualitatively distinct role. The conversational format of LLM chatbots (e.g., OpenAI’s ChatGPT or Google’s Gemini) can render observation highly salient, particularly through promotional framing of persistent memory functions. More recent implementations, such as Moltbot, further amplify this salience by incorporating structured “system files” (e.g., memory.md) that are explicitly described as mechanisms through which the system stores, updates, and utilizes information.

At the same time, observation in these environments is not uniformly explicit. Although chatbots and digital assistants may occasionally refer directly to prior interactions, thereby making their memory capabilities salient in conversation, personalization is often embedded more subtly. As a result, observation can appear simultaneously more salient and less salient, insofar as adaptive outputs are seamlessly integrated into conversational responses.

Users of such systems frequently deliberate on both the advantages and the potential drawbacks of such adaptive learning environments. For instance, discussions often address the risk of “over-prompting,” whereby extensive prior instructions may lead the model to generate overly specific or constrained outputs. Conversely, users also report situations in which digital assistants interpret instructions too broadly and apply them in contexts that were not intended. (add some links to discussions)

Moreover, particularly in chatbot-based interactions, system outputs may draw on information from conversations that fall outside the user’s immediate focus. While this can facilitate the integration of knowledge across interactions, it may also prove disruptive when previously discussed topics are unrelated to the current task, diluting the relevance of the response.

Salience of Algorithmic Profiles

  • Summary
  • Questions
  • AI mediated Environments

Although most consumers are generally aware that recommender systems are embedded in nearly all digital platforms, they are typically confronted with the precision—or lack thereof—of their algorithmic profiles only in specific situations.

First, salience arises when the algorithm appears to know the consumer too well. Highly accurate predictions are frequently experienced as unsettling or “creepy” (Zhang et al. 2025; Ham 2017), particularly when inferred personality profiles appear to migrate across platforms (Ham 2017).

Both overly accurate profiling and profiling that is entirely inconsistent with the self can elicit discomfort (Zhang et al. 2025). Resultingly, second, salience emerges when the algorithmic profile is clearly misaligned with consumers’ self-perceptions. Instances in which algorithms are markedly off base tend to evoke stronger feelings of not being seen or understood (Ruckenstein and Granroth 2020).

Third, algorithmic profiling becomes salient when external events draw attention to personalization practices. Public controversies, such as the Facebook personalization debate (Tucker 2014), or the temporary increase in privacy concerns following the Snowden disclosures, illustrate that consumers do reflect on the fact that they are continuously surveilled—but primarily when this surveillance is made explicitly visible. To some extent, the first case can likewise be interpreted as a form of externally triggered salience of algorithmic profiling.

AI-mediated environments such as chatbots and digital assistants introduce the comparatively distinctive feature of inviting users to actively participate in the construction of their own algorithmic profiles. Discussions in online forums indicate that users frequently reflect on what “their” LLM “knows” about them, and conversations about optimizing system outputs often emphasize the strategic provision of personal information as part of the input (for example stating preferences about dietary habits when asking for recipe suggestions, or pre-existing experience when generating code). In this sense, effective interaction is as the deliberate disclosure and calibration of self-relevant information.

This process of self-construction becomes even more pronounced in certain digital assistants. For example, MoltBot explicitly incorporates a “user description” file that is used to tailor interactions and actions to the individual (user.md). Notably, this user.md file can be populated directly by the user, but may also be generated and dynamically updated by the assistant itself. Such configurations formalize the notion of an evolving, system-readable representation of the user, thereby rendering the algorithmic profile both an object of user input and an autonomous site of system inference and modification.

Formation of Algorithmic Folk Theories

  • Summary
  • Questions
  • AI mediated Environments

Consumers’ reactions to algorithmic recommendations are shaped by their folk theories about how these systems operate. Rather than interpreting recommendations as outputs of statistical or mathematical models, consumers rely on intuitive, experience-based explanations of how algorithms function (DeVito, Gergle, and Birnholtz 2017; Rader and Gray 2015). These folk theories provide a cognitive framework through which algorithmic outputs are interpreted and evaluated.

Such interpretations can be situated within the Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM) (Friestad and Wright 1994). When consumers infer persuasive intent or strategic curation, they activate persuasion knowledge and adjust their responses accordingly, as demonstrated in the context of online behavioral advertising (Ham 2017). Thus, algorithmic outputs are not evaluated in isolation but filtered through pre-existing beliefs about system motives, data use, and personalization practices.

Folk theories about LLMs are widespread and frequently misaligned with the systems’ actual functioning. For example, prompts such as “make no mistakes” are sometimes interpreted as mechanisms that ensure factual accuracy, reflecting the assumption that the model can be instructed into guaranteed truthfulness. Such interactions illustrate the occasionally problematic mental models consumers bring to AI-mediated systems.

Many chatbots further reinforce these interpretive frameworks by offering system-level prompts that allow users to configure the assistant’s tone, style, or “personality,” thereby fostering the impression that one can construct “one’s own” LLM with a distinctive character. Online discussions likewise reveal extensive engagement with notions of emergent personalities and the personification of LLMs, with users attributing stable traits, dispositions, or even intentions to these systems.

In digital assistant ecosystems, this logic is formalized in artifacts such as “soul.md” files, which are described as specifying the assistant’s “soul” or enduring character. Across these contexts, a common feature of folk theories surrounding AI-mediated environments is the pronounced anthropomorphization of the underlying algorithms. This tendency appears markedly stronger than in popular conceptions of more traditional recommender systems, which are typically understood in more instrumental and less personified terms.

The Interplay between Algorithmic Representation of the Self and Perceived Self

  • Summary
  • Question
  • AI mediated Environments

The locus of epistemic authority over one’s preferences plays a central role in shaping consumer responses to algorithmic recommendations. Consumers may align their preferences with algorithmic suggestions—reflecting sentiments such as “Amazon knows what I want before I do” (see also Brown (2025))—or, conversely, they may attempt to align the algorithm with their pre-existing preferences [e.g., Anne’s unpublished paper; Rifkin and Etkin (2019)]

These positions do not represent a dichotomous choice but rather two ends of a continuum. Movement along this continuum is likely not always conscious or deliberate, as also evidenced by Adomavicius et al. (2013): consumers adjust their preferences in response to an algorithm’s prediction even when that prediction is not tailored to them. This finding suggests that consumers, at least to some extent, grant epistemic authority to algorithmic outputs, treating them as informative about their own preferences.

The conversational format of chatbots and digital assistants, combined with the pronounced tendency to anthropomorphize these systems (see also research on folk theories), frequently leads users to describe interactions in terms of the system “knowing” things about them. In some instances, users attribute context-dependent epistemic authority to the model, allowing it to guide or even make decisions on their behalf. These decisions range from relatively minor, everyday choices (e.g., “What should I have for dinner?”) to more consequential life considerations (e.g., “Should I quit my job and pursue a PhD?”). Such patterns suggest that LLMs are not merely treated as informational tools, but at times as advisory agents endowed with judgment.

At the same time, when LLMs mischaracterize a user’s preferences, values, or personality, reactions can be marked by strong discomfort or reactance. Misalignment at the level of perceived identity may be experienced as particularly intrusive, potentially eliciting stronger negative responses than comparable inaccuracies in traditional recommender systems, where personalization is typically less dialogical and less explicitly tied to the user’s self-concept.

In digital assistant contexts, these dynamics are further reflected in ongoing debates regarding control over persistent system files (e.g., user.md, soul.md). Some users argue that such representations should be populated and curated exclusively by the consumer, preserving individual authority over the construction of the algorithmic profile. Others assign greater autonomy to the LLM, endorsing its capacity to update and refine these representations over time. These disagreements underscore broader tensions concerning epistemic authority, agency, and ownership in AI-mediated identity construction.
