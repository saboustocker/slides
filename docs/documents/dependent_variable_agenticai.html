Actual-Ideal Preference Discrepancies

Both understandings of actual and ideal preference / self-concept are prevalent in literature: The understanding that actual self-concept / preference and ideal self-concept / preference are independent from each other, meaning that ideal self is not a state relative to the actual self, and that the actual self-concept / preference and ideal self-concept / preference are inherently linked to each other, meaning that the ideal self must be an idealized state relative to the actual self. It appears that the choice which definition to use primarily depends on the research question asked, and I was not able to identify big philosophical debates why one could be better or worse than the other.

Prevalent theories relating to self-concept / preferences and identity-based consumption can broadly be categorized into these two aspects:

Discrepancy Perspective

  • Higgins (1987) conceptualizes the self-discrepancy between actual-ideal, actual-ought and ought-ideal as relative to a personal reference-point, meaning that any effects from discrepancies only occur if the difference is large enough, not merely because ideal selves / preferences are high on an absolute scale.
  • Mandel et al. (2017) argues that self-discrepancies can motivate consumption as compensation

Congruity Perspective

  • M. Joseph Sirgy (1982) conceptualizes the ideal self is “the image of oneself as one would like to be” and ideal congruity is the match between a product/user image and that ideal self-image. He does not explicitly declare this, but from his descriptions, it can be implied that he sees ideal self and actual self as absolute self-images, not relative images to each other. Sirgy explicitly models actual- and ideal-self in relation to product congruence (\(\sum_i|P_{ij}-I_{ij}|\) and \(\sum_i|P_{ij}-S_{ij}|\), wherein \(P\) is product attributes, \(S\) is actual self, and \(I\) is ideal self).
  • Belk (2013), Belk (1988) argue that possessions become part of the extended self, wherein consumers should shed or neglect possessions is when possessions no longer fit consumers’ ideal self-images. This can occur either because the ideal self-image has changed or because the images of the objects formerly incorporated in extended self have changed. [Belk (1988); p. 159]

(2011) Congruity Ideal self-congruence is conceptualized on an independent scale to actual self-congruence. “Does this product match how I actually see myself?” and “how I ideally see myself?” without referencing the other anchor point. Therefore, ideal self-congruence is conceptualized on an independent scale to actual self-congruence.
Based on M. J. Sirgy et al. (1997).
Javornik et al. (2021) Discrepancy They measure actual–ideal attractiveness discrepancy as the gap between actual attractiveness and ideal attractiveness. Actual attractiveness: I am extremely attractive / extremely beautiful / extremely good-looking / extremely pretty.

Ideal attractiveness: Ideally, I would like to be extremely attractive / extremely beautiful / extremely good-looking / extremely pretty.

Discrepancy: gap between the two measures.
Based on Heine & Leghan (1990).
Ayoubi, Fazl, and Araam (2024) Congruity Uses two separate scales for actual and ideal self and defines the actual self as the “current self-perception” and the ideal self as the “traits and attributes an individual aspires to possess.” Brand personality scale by Aaker (1997). They operationalize actual and ideal self-concept independently from each other.
Kaur and Anand (2021) Congruity They look at actual vs. ideal preferences in the context of fashion congruity, but do not analyze the gap between actual and ideal fashion self-congruity. Actual Fashion Self-congruity: The extent to which an individual perceives his/her fashion style to be a representation of attributes that he/she believes he actually possesses.

Ideal Fashion Self-congruity: The extent to which an individual perceives his/her fashion style to be a representation of attributes that he/she aspires to possess.
Self-developed.
Zogaj, Mähner, and Tscheulin (2024) Congruity They base their items on several other sources, but make their own items. Their item formulation strongly implies a discrepancy/difference for the ideal self, but they do not explicitly state that in their paper. The personality of the “ABC/Mercedes” brand is consistent with how I see myself (my actual self).
The personality of the “ABC/Mercedes” brand is a mirror image of me (my actual self).

The personality of the “ABC/Mercedes” brand is consistent with how I would like to be (my ideal self).
The personality of the “ABC/Mercedes” brand is a mirror image of the person I would like to be (my ideal self).

Operationalizing Actual vs. Ideal Preferences when it comes to Dietary Habits

Options in Operationalizing

All options listed are based on the discussions we had previously. I hope I didn’t miss anything.

Input: Open-ended vs. Predefined

Option Advantages Disadvantages
Open-ended More individualized; participants are more likely to identify with the foods they list; higher personal relevance. Higher risk of consistency bias: participants may avoid self-conflict and claim that the foods they listed are also what they ideally want.
Predefined list More generalizable and comparable across participants; easier to analyze systematically. Lower personal investment; lists can become long quickly; some participants may not identify with specific products.

Open-ended Input Options

Option Advantages Disadvantages
List n foods they would like to eat Simple and personally meaningful; captures foods participants actively desire. Does not clearly distinguish actual preferences from ideal preferences.
List current foods + foods to eat more/less of Directly separates actual behavior from desired change; better suited for a discrepancy perspective. More complex; may make actual–ideal conflict more salient.

Evaluation: Independent vs. Relative Actual–Ideal Preferences

Option Advantages Disadvantages
Treat actual and ideal preferences independently Simpler design; can test whether one profile is more ideal-congruent than another. Cannot show whether ideal preferences differ from actual preferences; high ideal-congruence may simply reflect already-ideal behavior.
Treat actual and ideal preferences relative to each other Allows estimating the actual–ideal discrepancy; directly tests whether profiles move closer to actual or ideal preferences. More complex design and analysis; requires measuring both actual and ideal preferences.

Discrepancy Measurement Options

Option Advantages Disadvantages
Two-phased design Cleanest separation between baseline preferences and profile construction; closest to a stated vs. revealed preference logic; can adapt Khambatta et al. (2023). Most complex; requires multiple survey stages or repeated contact.
One-phased design Simpler and easier to implement; avoids needing a separate pre-survey. Higher risk of post-hoc consistency bias: participants may rate their own profile as ideal because they just created it.
Rank-order / selection design Simple behavioral indicator; lower rating burden; easy to quantify as share of actual vs. ideal foods selected. Less precise than continuous ratings; depends heavily on how the actual and ideal food pools are constructed.

Based on our research question, I think we should adopt a discrepancy perspective on actual and ideal preferences. We hypothesize that consumers construct different profiles depending on whether the system is agentic or just recommends.

To test this, we need to measure both actual and ideal preferences. If we only measure ideal preferences, we can show whether a profile is ideal-congruent, but we cannot determine whether it is more ideal than actual, nor whether agentic and recommender systems shift profile construction in opposite directions. The prediction is comparative: a profile may be close to the ideal self, close to the actual self, or somewhere between the two. Without measuring the actual baseline, we cannot estimate this distance.

For example, suppose a participant says they ideally want to eat more vegetables. If we only measure this ideal preference, and their AI profile includes many vegetables, we might conclude that the profile is ideal-congruent. However, this conclusion is incomplete if the participant already eats many vegetables in everyday life. In that case, the profile may not reflect an ideal shift at all; it may simply reproduce the actual baseline.

Therefore, measuring both actual and ideal preferences allows us to identify the actual–ideal discrepancy and test whether system type changes which side of the discrepancy consumers orient toward. This is essential because our theoretical claim concerns preference alignment relative to a baseline, not ideal preferences in isolation.
