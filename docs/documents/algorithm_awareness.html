Algorithm Awareness

Summary of related literature and exploratory survey design

Author

Sabou Rani Stocker

Published

February 15, 2026

Living in a digital world, we interact with vast amounts of data every day. By the end of 2025, Instagram was estimated to have 34 billion posts uploaded in that year alone (Go Beyond 2024). Amazon lists approximately 350 million products (Capital One Shopping 2025), and on YouTube about 360 hours of content are uploaded every minute (moodive 2025). Given this scale, consumers cannot realistically navigate these systems without algorithmic recommendation. Digital environments are therefore structured by algorithmic curation: From social media feeds to product recommendations, consumers rarely encounter neutral choice sets; instead, they interact with selections shaped by systems that continuously learn from their past behavior. While recommender systems research primarily evaluates prediction accuracy and optimization performance (e.g., Garapati and Chakraborty 2025; Raza et al. 2026), far less attention has been paid to the cognitive and behavioral impact on consumers:

What happens when consumers know that algorithms are observing and learning from them?

Folk Theories about (Algorithmic) Persuasion and Recommendation

Most people are aware that their online experience is strongly shaped by algorithmic curation and recommendation. “The algorithm” is a regular topic of discussion (and disdain) on various online platforms [Munger (2024); r/youtubers (2026); and many more]. It is however also apparent that few consumers’ understanding of “the algorithm” is rooted in the mathematical bases recommender systems are built on (DeVito, Gergle, and Birnholtz 2017; Rader and Gray 2015). One possible explanation is rooted in the literature on persuasion knowledge in consumer research, and related streams of literature applying this concept to recommender systems.

The Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM) formalizes how consumers’ beliefs about persuasion attempts shape their responses (Friestad and Wright (1994)). In this framework, consumers (“targets”) and marketers (“agents”) each draw on topic knowledge, persuasion knowledge, and knowledge about the other party. Persuasion knowledge comprises beliefs about psychological mediators, marketer tactics, one’s own coping strategies, the effectiveness and appropriateness of tactics, and both parties’ goals. Crucially, these beliefs are subjective and vary across individuals and contexts (Friestad and Wright (1994)).

This framework can be extended to an algorithmic persuasion knowledge model (aPKM). Although algorithms differ from human marketers, their functional goal is comparable: encouraging desired behaviors such as scrolling, clicking, sharing, or purchasing. Folk theories about algorithmic persuasion are widespread. Rader and Gray (2015) analyze intuitive theories about Facebook’s newsfeed algorithm, while DeVito, Gergle, and Birnholtz (2017) show that inaccurate beliefs about algorithms shape resistance to algorithmic change. Moreover, making different recommendation rationales salient (e.g., similarity to other users vs. similarity to items) alters engagement behavior (Gai and Klesse (2019)).

Together, these findings suggest that consumers’ subjective beliefs about algorithms; whether they see them as observers, persuaders, or epistemic authorities; shape how algorithmic pre-selection influences autonomy, preference construction, and behavioral responses.

Feeling Perceived by the Algorithm

Folk theories about how algorithms work build the basis on which consumers construct their awareness of algorithmic recommendation and presence, and how they adapt their behavior accordingly. This modified behavior however presumes that consumers feel “perceived by the algorithm” at all.

While recommender systems research typically focuses on optimizing algorithmic performance and improving prediction accuracy and customer retention (Garapati and Chakraborty 2025; Raza et al. 2026), consumer research has examined how individuals respond to algorithmic pre-selection. Work in this domain investigates reactance to algorithmic curation, consumers’ folk theories about algorithms (e.g., their presumed inability to weigh relative importance of preferences; Anne), and the ways in which algorithmic cues influence judgments. For instance, consumers anchor their product evaluations on recommender system outputs when these are made explicit: Adomavicius et al. (2013) show that participants adjust their own ratings toward an algorithm’s predicted rating (“Our algorithm thinks that you would rate the show you are about to see as 1.5/5.”, p. 962). These anchoring effects occur even when the recommendation stems from a fake algorithm, indicating that actual knowledge about the consumer is not necessary to influence perceived ratings.

Feeling Observed changes how we Act and Decide

A substantial body of research in social psychology, consumer research, and marketing has examined the effects of observation—or even the mere presence of others—on decision-making (e.g., Zwebner and Schrift (2020)). In general, individuals show aversion to the feeling of being observed (Zwebner and Schrift (2020)), particularly when observation threatens their sense of autonomy. The question is whether such findings extend to algorithms: do personalized recommendations elicit a feeling that one’s past actions have been observed?

There is evidence that perceived observation reduces felt decision autonomy, for instance by increasing pressure to conform to defaults (Zwebner and Schrift (2020)). Similarly, perceived autonomy over data sharing affects behavioral responses: although the ad-targeting mechanism remained unchanged, when consumers felt they had control over how much personal information they displayed, click-through rates doubled (from .1% to .25%) (Tucker (2014)). Thus, perceived control—rather than objective system properties—shapes consumer reactions.

Is Algorithmic Pre-Selection Perceived as a Reduction of Autonomy?

The cognitve and behavioral reactions to feeling seen by an algorithm may even go further than situational reactions, and influence how consumers perceive their fundamental autonomy in chosing their digital experience.

If algorithmic personalization is experienced as observation, it may also be perceived as constraining autonomy. Research shows that observation can undermine decision autonomy (Zwebner and Schrift (2020)). This raises the possibility that algorithmic pre-selection is interpreted as narrowing one’s choice space or subtly pressuring certain decisions.

At the same time, consumers do not uniformly attribute strong knowledge or authority to algorithms. Discussions about who “knows” the consumer better—the consumer or the algorithm—span multiple domains. Popular narratives suggest that platforms such as Amazon can detect pregnancy before individuals do themselves (source), and philosophical discussions propose that algorithmic curation may shape identity (“If Instagram shows me x content, I must be x.”; Brown (2025)).

However, other evidence suggests skepticism toward algorithmic knowledge: people often perceive algorithms as less capable of truly knowing who they are (Anne’s unpublished study; algorithm aversion literature). Thus, algorithms may simultaneously be seen as powerful observers and as epistemically limited systems.

Who Has the Final Ownership of Knowledge?

This loss of autonomy and shaping of an experience “by the algorithm” might even have more fundamental effects affecting the perceived sense of identity or self by the consumer. Literature suggesting both directions - feeling threatened by a mismatch in self-perception and algorithmic recommendation, as well as updating one’s own ideas and beliefs based on algorithmic recommendation, have been discussed in literature.

The tension between perceived algorithmic knowledge and personal self-knowledge becomes particularly relevant when considering the relationship between choices and preferences. Research on self-control shows that exerting self-control weakens the perceived correspondence between choice and preference (Sela, Berger, and Kim (2017)). For example, if one exerts self-control to avoid wearing pajamas to the office, wearing a suit feels less preference-consistent. These effects spill over into unrelated domains, such as food choices.

Importantly, lay theories about self-control moderate this relationship. Most individuals view self-control as acting against the self (overriding impulses), while a minority perceive it as acting with the self (enabling long-term goals). Only the former group exhibits weakened choice–preference correspondence when self-control is salient (Sela, Berger, and Kim (2017), Study 3).

In algorithmic contexts, this raises the question of whether consumers interpret algorithmic suggestions as aligned with their “true” preferences or as external pressures acting against the self. Studies such as Rifkin and Etkin (2019) suggest that consumers’ see algorithmic deviation from their perceived concept of (stable) self as undesireable and unpleasant, and furthermore also as going against their perceived self.
